Class 9 Chemistry Chapter 18 Notes — Introduction to Analytical Chemistry NBF Syllabus

Syllabus: National Book Foundation (NBF) / FBISE & Punjab Boards | Academic Year: 2026

Chapter Overview: Chapter 18 introduces Analytical Chemistry, the science of identifying and quantifying substances. It covers qualitative and quantitative analysis, standard gas identification tests, and flame tests used to detect specific elements and compounds.

Key Topics in This Chapter

  • Qualitative analysis vs quantitative analysis
  • Gas identification tests: ammonia, CO2, oxygen, hydrogen, SO2
  • Flame tests and characteristic flame colours of metals
  • Oxidizing flame vs reducing flame: differences and uses
  • Origin of flame colour through electron energy transitions
  • Litmus paper tests and limewater test for CO2

Short Answer Questions

Q(i). Ammonia detection with litmus paper.

Answer: Ammonia gas ($NH_3$) exhibits an alkaline/basic nature in chemical properties. When a strip of moist red litmus paper is carefully exposed to an unknown stream of ammonia gas, the alkaline molecules dissolve in the paper's moisture film and turn the indicators deep blue, successfully confirming the presence of ammonia.

Q(ii). Identification of carbon dioxide.

Answer: Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) can be identified by bubbling the unknown gas directly through a test tube containing clear limewater [aqueous calcium hydroxide, $Ca(OH)_2$]. If $CO_2$ is present, it reacts to form an insoluble white precipitate of calcium carbonate ($CaCO_3$), which turns the limewater notably milky.

Q(iii). Detection of Oxygen gas.

Answer: Oxygen gas ($O_2$) strongly supports and accelerates combustion reactions, though it is not self-flammable. It can be identified by introducing a glowing wooden splint into a sample container; upon contact with pure $O_2$, the dying ember will immediately rekindle and burst back into a bright active flame.

Q(iv). Detection of Sulphur dioxide gas.

Answer: Sulphur dioxide ($SO_2$) gas can be identified using an aqueous solution of acidified potassium permanganate ($KMnO_4$). When $SO_2$ gas bubbles through this mixture, its strong reducing properties break down the compound, causing the distinct purple coloration of the solution to turn completely decolored and clear.

Q(v). Difference between oxidizing and reducing flame.

Answer: The core differences between these two operational settings of a Bunsen burner are:

  • Oxidizing Flame: Produced when the burner's air collar is fully opened, providing a high oxygen supply. It burns with a highly efficient, hot, non-luminous blue outer zone and is mainly used for oxidation experiments and high-heat tasks.
  • Reducing Flame: Produced when the air intake is closed or limited. This leaves unburnt carbon gases, creating a cooler, luminous yellow inner zone used for reduction tests and heating metals without causing surface oxidation.

Long Answer Questions / Detailed Explanations

Q3. Difference between qualitative and quantitative analysis.

Answer: Qualitative and quantitative analysis represent the two fundamental pillars of analytical chemistry:

Feature Qualitative Analysis Quantitative Analysis
Primary Focus Determines what chemical components, ions, or functional groups are present in a sample. Determines how much or the exact structural mass percentage of a specific component is present.
Data Type Descriptive observation data (e.g., color changes, gas evolution, precipitate formation). Numerical and statistical measurements (e.g., mass in grams, molar concentrations, volumes).
Lab Example Detecting the presence of ammonia gas or iron cations in an unknown salt mixture sample. Performing acid-base titrations to calculate the exact molar concentration of an acid solution.

Q4. Origin of flame colour.

Answer: Flame colors originate from quantum electronic transitions within metal atoms or ions when heated in a high-temperature Bunsen burner flame.

When a metal salt sample is introduced into the flame, the thermal energy is absorbed by the metal's valence electrons, causing them to jump from their stable ground state energy levels up to unstable, temporary excited states.

Because these excited states are highly unstable, the electrons quickly drop back down to their original lower ground state energy configurations. As they fall back, they release their captured energy as electromagnetic radiation (photons). For many alkali and alkaline earth metals, the energy gap corresponds perfectly to specific light wavelengths within the visible spectrum, producing characteristic flame colors (such as sodium's golden yellow or copper's blue-green).

Q5. Why hydrogen gas produces a popping sound in flame.

Answer: Hydrogen gas ($H_2$) is an exceptionally combustible element with a very low activation energy requirement. When a small concentration or pocket of pure hydrogen gas is exposed to an open flame in the presence of atmospheric oxygen, it undergoes a rapid, highly exothermic combustion reaction, forming water vapor according to the chemical equation:

2H₂ (g) + O₂ (g) → 2H₂O (g) + Heat energy

This rapid reaction causes a localized shockwave due to the sudden expansion of gas molecules from the intense heat. This miniature pressure explosion is heard in the laboratory as a distinct, sharp "pop" sound, serving as a reliable qualitative test for hydrogen.

Q6. Why litmus paper turns white after turning red when exposed to Sulphur dioxide gas.

Answer: This two-stage color transformation occurs due to the sequential acidic and reducing chemical reactions that sulphur dioxide ($SO_2$) undergoes on moist litmus paper:

  1. The Red Transformation: When $SO_2$ gas comes into contact with the damp blue litmus paper, it immediately dissolves in the water droplets present on the fibers to form sulfurous acid ($H_2SO_3$), an acidic solution. This acid alters the pH indicator compound, turning the blue paper red.
  2. The White Transformation (Bleaching): After forming the acid, the excess $SO_2$ acts as a powerful reducing bleaching agent. It reacts with the moisture to release nascent hydrogen atoms, which attack and reduce the organic dye molecules within the litmus paper, breaking down their chromophore structures. This process completely strips the pigment away, turning the paper white.

Exam Tips for Chapter 18

For Analytical Chemistry, memorizing the flame test colors for different metal cations is crucial for MCQs. Additionally, make sure you can describe the qualitative tests for common gases like hydrogen, oxygen, and carbon dioxide, as these appear frequently in short questions.
