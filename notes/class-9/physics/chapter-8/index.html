Short Questions

Q1. Can two magnetic field lines intersect each other? Justify your answer.

No, two magnetic field lines cannot intersect each other. At the point of intersection, the magnetic field would have two directions at the same point, which is physically impossible.

Q2. A freely suspended magnet always points along the north-south direction. Why?

A freely suspended magnet aligns itself with Earth's magnetic field. Since Earth's magnetic field runs roughly from south to north, the magnet always points in the north-south direction.

Q3. What is the neutral zone or field-free region of the magnetic field?

The neutral zone is a region where two magnetic fields cancel each other completely, so the resultant magnetic field becomes zero at that point.

Q4. Is there any material which does not have any magnetic behavior? Justify your answer.

No, every material shows some magnetic behavior at the atomic level, because all matter is made of electrons that spin and orbit. However, in many materials this effect is extremely weak and difficult to detect under normal conditions.

Q5. A proton also spins like an electron. Why is its magnetic effect neglected in the study of magnetism?

The magnetic effect of a proton is neglected because its mass is about 1836 times greater than that of an electron. Due to its much larger mass, it spins far more slowly, producing a negligibly small magnetic effect compared to an electron.

Q6. What is the geomagnetic reversal phenomenon? Explain.

Geomagnetic reversal is the phenomenon in which Earth's magnetic poles swap their positions — the north magnetic pole becomes the south magnetic pole and vice versa. This has happened multiple times over millions of years in Earth's history.

Q7. Why does Earth spin about its geographical axis instead of its magnetic axis?

Earth spins about its geographical axis because rotation depends on the distribution of mass and gravitational forces acting on the planet — not on the magnetic field. The magnetic axis is produced by fluid motion in the core, which is independent of rotational axis.

Q8. Why are Earth's geographical and magnetic axes not coincident?

Earth's magnetic field is produced by the movement of molten iron and nickel in its liquid outer core. This motion is not perfectly aligned with the rotational axis, so the magnetic axis is tilted by about 11° to 12° away from the geographical axis.

Q9. What is the difference between paramagnetic and ferromagnetic materials?

Paramagnetic materials are only weakly attracted by a magnetic field and do not retain magnetism when the field is removed (e.g., aluminum, oxygen). Ferromagnetic materials are very strongly attracted and can become permanent magnets (e.g., iron, nickel, cobalt).

Q10. On what factors does the strength of the magnetic field of an electromagnet depend?

The strength of an electromagnet depends on three factors:

  • The amount of electric current flowing through the coil
  • The number of turns in the coil
  • The type of core material used (soft iron gives the strongest field)

Q11. Draw magnetic field lines of two solenoids placed near each other: (i) facing same poles, (ii) facing opposite poles.

(i) Same Poles Facing Each Other: The magnetic field lines repel each other and spread outward between the two solenoids, creating a field-free neutral zone between them.

(ii) Opposite Poles Facing Each Other: The magnetic field lines attract each other and join smoothly from one solenoid to the other, forming a combined uniform field between them — similar to a single bar magnet.

Long Questions

Q1. Define and explain magnetism.

Magnetism: Magnetism is a natural force of attraction or repulsion between objects caused by the motion of electric charges (electrons).

Key Points:

  • Magnetism originates at the atomic level due to the spin of electrons around their own axes.
  • Every magnet has two poles: a North Pole and a South Pole. These poles cannot be separated.
  • Like poles repel each other; opposite poles attract each other.
  • The region around a magnet where magnetic force can be detected is called the magnetic field.

Q2. What is the domain theory of magnetism? Explain.

Magnetic Domain: Inside magnetic metals, atoms group together in tiny microscopic regions called magnetic domains. Each domain acts like a small independent magnet with its own north and south pole.

Unmagnetized State: In a normal piece of iron, the domains point in all different random directions, so their magnetic effects cancel each other out and the overall material shows no magnetism.

Magnetized State: When an external magnet is brought near, all the domains rotate and line up in the same direction, turning the metal into a strong magnet. The more domains that align, the stronger the magnetism.

Q3. Explain magnetic field strength and magnetic shielding.

Magnetic Field Strength: The intensity or concentration of a magnetic field at a specific point. It is strongest near the poles where field lines are crowded together, and weakest far away where lines are spread apart.
Magnetic Shielding: A method used to protect sensitive electronic devices from external magnetic fields. High-permeability materials like soft iron are placed around the device. The soft iron redirects the magnetic field lines around the protected area instead of through it.

Common uses of magnetic shielding include protecting MRI machines, sensitive scientific instruments, and electronic components from unwanted magnetic interference.

Q4. Explain the magnetic field of a bar magnet and that of a solenoid. Also compare them.

Bar Magnet: A permanent magnet with fixed North and South poles. Its field lines loop out from the North pole, curve around the outside, and re-enter at the South pole. The field exists at all times without any external source of energy.

Solenoid: A cylindrical coil of wire that creates a uniform, strong magnetic field inside itself only when an electric current flows through it. Outside the solenoid, the field pattern looks almost identical to that of a bar magnet.

Magnet TypePermanent magnetTemporary magnet
Field StrengthFixed — cannot be changedVariable — changed by current or number of turns

Q5. Define induced magnetism. Explain methods of induced magnetism.

Induced Magnetism: Induced magnetism is temporary magnetism acquired by a magnetic material (like an iron nail) when it is placed inside or near a magnetic field. The material becomes a magnet itself without being physically stroked or electrified permanently.

Methods of Inducing Magnetism:

  • Proximity / Touch Method: Bringing a permanent magnet close to or in contact with an unmagnetized iron object. The object becomes temporarily magnetized as its domains align with the external field.
  • Stroking Method: Rubbing an unmagnetized steel bar repeatedly in one direction with the same pole of a permanent magnet. Each stroke aligns more domains in the same direction, gradually producing stronger magnetism.
  • Electrical Method (Quickest): Placing a metal bar inside a solenoid coil and passing Direct Current (DC) through the wire. The strong uniform field inside the solenoid aligns all domains rapidly and completely.

Q7. Explain some uses of electromagnets and temporary magnets.

  • Lifting Cranes: Large electromagnets are used in scrap yards to pick up and drop heavy metal objects by switching electricity on and off.
  • Electric Bells: Use a temporary magnet to repeatedly pull a clapper to strike a bell whenever current flows through the circuit.
  • Maglev Trains: Powerful electromagnets levitate and propel trains above the track without any physical friction, allowing very high speeds.
  • Speakers: An electromagnet interacts with a permanent magnet to convert electrical audio signals into physical vibrations that produce sound.
  • MRI Machines: Use very strong electromagnets to produce detailed images of the inside of the human body.

Q8. Explain the three types of magnetic materials.

Diamagnetic Materials: Materials that are weakly repelled by a magnet. They have no unpaired electrons. Examples: Water, Copper, Gold, Bismuth.
Paramagnetic Materials: Materials that are weakly attracted by a magnet. They have some unpaired electrons but do not retain magnetism. Examples: Aluminum, Oxygen, Platinum.
Ferromagnetic Materials: Materials that are very strongly attracted by a magnet and can be permanently magnetized due to strong domain alignment. Examples: Iron, Nickel, Cobalt.

Q9. Explain the Earth's magnetic field. Also relate Earth's geographical and magnetic poles.

Earth's Magnetic Field: The Earth acts like a giant magnet. This is caused by circulating electrical currents of molten iron and nickel in its outer liquid core. These currents generate a strong magnetic field that extends out into space, forming the magnetosphere.

Relation Between Geographical and Magnetic Poles:

  • Earth's magnetic axis is tilted by about 11° to 12° from its geographic axis.
  • The geographic North Pole actually houses Earth's magnetic south pole — which is why the north-seeking end of a compass needle (which is a north pole) is attracted to it.
  • Earth's magnetic field intensity varies from place to place and is not constant everywhere.

Q10. Explain how birds and other migrating animals use Earth's magnetic field to navigate.

  • Internal Compass (Magnetite): Tiny magnetic mineral crystals (magnetite) found in the beaks or skulls of birds act like tiny compass needles, sending directional signals to their brains.
  • Eyesight Compass (Cryptochromes): Special proteins called cryptochromes in birds' eyes allow them to visually perceive the angle of Earth's magnetic field lines as patterns of light and shadow.
  • Inclination Mapping: Animals sense how the magnetic field lines tilt relative to Earth's surface. This allows them to determine their exact geographic position and navigate accurately across thousands of kilometers.

Numerical Problem with Solution

Q1. Find the magnetic field due to a wire at a distance of 10 cm, if 1.3 A current is passing through the wire.

Given:
Current (I) = 1.3 A
Distance (r) = 10 cm = 0.1 m
Permeability of free space (μ₀) = 4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A

Formula: B = (μ₀ × I) / (2π × r)

Step 1: Substitute values:
B = (4π × 10⁻⁷ × 1.3) / (2π × 0.1)

Step 2: Cancel 2π from numerator and denominator:
B = (2 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.3) / 0.1

Step 3: Multiply numerator:
B = (2.6 × 10⁻⁷) / 0.1

Step 4: Divide by 0.1:
B = 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ T

Note: The textbook prints 2.6 × 10⁻⁵ T, which appears to be a printing error in the exponent. The correct calculated answer for r = 0.1 m is 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ T.
✅ Final Answer: B = 2.6 × 10⁻⁶ T
