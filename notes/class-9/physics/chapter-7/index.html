Class 9 Physics Chapter 7 Notes — Properties of Matter NBF Syllabus

Chapter Overview: Chapter 7 of Class 9 Physics (NBF Syllabus) covers Properties of Matter — a chapter combining concepts of density, states of matter, plasma, internal energy, and thermometry. Students learn how to measure density of solids, liquids, and gases, distinguish between the three states of matter at the particle level, understand what plasma is, and study different types of thermometers including liquid-in-glass, resistance, thermocouple, and gas thermometers. The chapter also covers how to modify thermometers for better sensitivity, responsiveness, and range. These topics appear frequently in MCQs and short questions in FBISE and Punjab Board exams.

Key Topics in This Chapter

  • Density — definition, formula, SI unit
  • Measuring density of regular solids, irregular solids, liquids, and gases
  • States of matter — solids, liquids, gases (particle-level comparison)
  • Plasma — the fourth state of matter
  • Internal energy — kinetic and potential energy of particles
  • Thermometric properties — thermal expansion of liquids, electrical resistance
  • Liquid-in-glass thermometer — construction and working
  • Constant-volume and constant-pressure gas thermometers
  • Resistance thermometer and thermocouple thermometer
  • Modifications to improve sensitivity, responsiveness, and range
  • Absolute zero — −273.15°C

Short Questions

Q1. Two liquids A and B have densities 1 g/mL and 1.2 g/mL respectively. Which liquid is on top, and why?

Liquid A will stay on top because it has lower density than liquid B. Less dense liquids float above denser liquids.

Q2. Write a method to find the volume and density of a human body.

The volume of a human body can be found by the water displacement method — submerge the body in a large tank and measure the water displaced. Density is then calculated by dividing mass by volume:

Density = Mass / Volume

Q3. How is plasma the fourth state of matter? Give a reason.

Plasma is called the fourth state of matter because it contains charged particles such as ions and electrons. It can conduct electricity and is found in stars, lightning, and neon lights.

Q4. Why is water not used in liquid-in-glass thermometers?

Water is not used because it expands irregularly, sticks to glass, and freezes at 0°C. Due to these properties, it cannot measure temperature accurately across the required range.

Q5. Can we increase the internal energy of a substance without increasing its temperature?

Yes, during changes of state like melting or boiling, internal energy increases without any rise in temperature because the energy is used to change the state (breaking bonds), not to increase motion.

Q6. Why are fixed point scales required for thermometers? What difficulties are there when setting fixed points?

Fixed points are required to make temperature measurements accurate and uniform everywhere. Difficulties occur because impurities and changes in pressure can affect the boiling and freezing points of the reference substance.

Q7. Mercury is replaced with alcohol in liquid-in-glass thermometers. Discuss the possible change in sensitivity and range.

The sensitivity increases because alcohol expands more than mercury for small temperature changes. However, the range decreases because alcohol boils at a lower temperature (78°C) than mercury (357°C).

Q8. Why is −273.15°C called absolute zero? Can we achieve this temperature?

It is called absolute zero because molecular motion becomes minimum (almost stops) at this temperature. It cannot be achieved completely in practice — it can only be approached closely in laboratory conditions.

Q9. Why is a thermocouple thermometer suitable to measure high temperatures but not a liquid-in-glass thermometer?

A thermocouple thermometer can measure very high temperatures because metals can resist high heat. A liquid-in-glass thermometer is not suitable because the liquid may boil or the glass may crack or break at very high temperatures.

Q10. Can we increase the sensitivity of a liquid-in-glass thermometer without changing its range?

Yes, sensitivity can be increased by using a narrow capillary tube and a larger bulb. This makes the liquid rise more for a small temperature change without changing the fixed points (range).

Q11. One student claims to have constructed a more sensitive liquid-in-glass thermometer. How can her claim be verified?

Her claim can be verified by comparing it with an ordinary thermometer. If a small temperature change causes a larger movement of the liquid column in her thermometer, then it is more sensitive.

Long Questions

Q1. Define density and describe methods to measure it for different types of substances.

Density: Density is defined as the mass of an object per unit volume. It tells us how tightly packed the matter inside an object is.
Formula: Density = Mass / Volume  |  SI Unit: kg/m³ or g/cm³

A. Regular-Shaped Solids (e.g., cubes, cylinders):

  • Find mass using an electronic balance.
  • Measure dimensions with a ruler or vernier calipers and calculate volume using geometric formulas.
  • Divide mass by volume to get density.

B. Irregular-Shaped Solids (displacement method):

  • Weigh the solid and record its mass.
  • Pour water into a measuring cylinder and record initial volume (V₁).
  • Submerge the solid completely and record new volume (V₂).
  • Volume of solid = V₂ − V₁. Then divide mass by this volume.

C. Liquids:

  • Weigh empty measuring cylinder (M₁). Add liquid and weigh again (M₂). Mass of liquid = M₂ − M₁.
  • Read volume from the cylinder markings. Divide mass by volume.

D. Gases:

  • Evacuate a glass flask using a vacuum pump, seal it, and weigh (M₁). Let gas fill the flask, weigh again (M₂). Mass of gas = M₂ − M₁.
  • Volume = internal volume of flask (found by filling with water). Divide mass by volume.

Q3. Describe physical properties that vary with temperature and how they are used in thermometers.

Property 1: Thermal Expansion of a Liquid

Most liquids expand when heated and contract when cooled. In a liquid-in-glass thermometer, mercury or colored alcohol is placed in a bulb connected to a narrow capillary tube. When heated, the liquid expands and rises up the tube. The height of the liquid column corresponds to calibrated temperature markings.

Property 2: Electrical Resistance of a Wire

As the temperature of a metal wire increases, atoms vibrate more violently, creating more obstacles for electrons — so electrical resistance increases with temperature. A resistance thermometer uses a fine platinum wire probe; an external circuit measures the resistance and converts it directly into a temperature reading.

Q4. Describe the construction and working of gas thermometers.

1. Constant-Volume Gas Thermometer:

  • Construction: A glass bulb filled with a fixed mass of gas connected to a U-tube mercury manometer. One side is open to atmosphere; a movable mercury reservoir adjusts levels.
  • Working: The bulb is placed in the substance being measured. Temperature increase → gas pressure increases → mercury is pushed down. The reservoir is raised until mercury returns to the original level. The difference in height (h) between the two arms gives pressure, which is proportional to absolute temperature.

2. Constant-Pressure Gas Thermometer:

  • Construction: A gas-filled bulb connected to a calibrated tube with a small mercury droplet acting as a movable index plug, keeping internal pressure equal to atmospheric pressure.
  • Working: When the bulb is heated, gas expands at constant pressure, pushing the mercury droplet along the scale. The position of the droplet gives the temperature reading.

Q5. How can a liquid-in-glass thermometer be modified to improve its sensitivity, responsiveness, and range?

A. To Improve Sensitivity (large movement for small temperature change):

  • Narrower capillary bore: Tiny volume expansion forces liquid to travel a long distance.
  • Larger bulb: More liquid = larger total expansion for the same temperature change.

B. To Improve Responsiveness (reacts quickly to temperature changes):

  • Thin-walled glass bulb: Less thermal resistance → heat reaches the liquid faster.
  • Cylindrical bulb: Greater surface area → faster heat transfer from surroundings.

C. To Optimize Range:

  • For low temperatures: use alcohol (freezing point −114°C).
  • For high temperatures: use mercury (boiling point 357°C).
  • Lengthening the stem accommodates a wider span of temperature markings.

Numerical Problems with Solutions

Q1. Sarah has a wooden block (mass 300 g, volume 150 cm³) and a metal ball (mass 500 g, volume 50 cm³). Calculate the density of each and determine which is denser.

Wooden Block:
Density = Mass / Volume = 300 / 150 = 2 g/cm³

Metal Ball:
Density = Mass / Volume = 500 / 50 = 10 g/cm³

Conclusion: The metal ball is denser (10 g/cm³ > 2 g/cm³).
✅ Wooden Block: 2 g/cm³  |  Metal Ball: 10 g/cm³  |  Metal ball is denser

Q2. A container has 500 mL of cooking oil with mass 450 g. Calculate the density of oil.

Given: m = 450 g, V = 500 mL

Formula: Density = m / V
Density = 450 / 500 = 0.9 g/mL
✅ Final Answer: 0.9 g/mL

Q3. A plastic box is 70 cm × 10 cm × 30 cm with mass 2500 g. Find the density of plastic.

Given: l = 70 cm, w = 10 cm, h = 30 cm, m = 2500 g

Step 1 — Volume:
V = 70 × 10 × 30 = 21,000 cm³

Step 2 — Density:
Density = 2500 / 21,000 ≈ 0.12 g/cm³
✅ Final Answer: 0.12 g/cm³

Q4. Aluminium has a density of 2700 kg/m³. Find the mass of a solid aluminium ball with diameter 25 cm.

Given: ρ = 2700 kg/m³, d = 25 cm, r = 12.5 cm = 0.125 m

Step 1 — Volume of sphere:
V = (4/3) × π × r³ = (4/3) × 3.1416 × (0.125)³
V ≈ 0.008181 m³

Step 2 — Mass:
Mass = ρ × V = 2700 × 0.008181 ≈ 22 kg
✅ Final Answer: 22 kg

Q5. A cube of iron has side length 10 cm and mass 0.5 kg. Density of iron is 7.8 g/cm³. Find the volume of the cavity inside.

Given: side = 10 cm, m = 0.5 kg = 500 g, ρ_iron = 7.8 g/cm³

Step 1 — Total geometric volume:
V_total = 10³ = 1000 cm³

Step 2 — Volume occupied by iron:
V_iron = m / ρ = 500 / 7.8 ≈ 64.1 cm³

Step 3 — Volume of cavity:
V_cavity = V_total − V_iron = 1000 − 64.1 = 935.9 cm³
✅ Final Answer: 935.9 cm³

Q6. Mass of an irregular stone is 200 g. Water level rises from 40 mL to 73 mL. Find the volume and density of the stone.

Given: m = 200 g, V₁ = 40 mL, V₂ = 73 mL

Step 1 — Volume by displacement:
V = V₂ − V₁ = 73 − 40 = 33 mL (33 cm³)

Step 2 — Density:
Density = m / V = 200 / 33 ≈ 6.1 g/mL
✅ Volume = 33 mL  |  Density ≈ 6.1 g/mL

⭐ Exam Tips for Chapter 7

The density formula (ρ = m/V) is the most tested numerical topic — always check units and convert grams/cm³ to kg/m³ when needed (1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³). For MCQs, remember that density is an intrinsic property — it does not change with the amount of substance. For theory, the particle-level comparison of solids, liquids, and gases is a popular short question. Also remember: sensitivity and range often have a trade-off in thermometer design.
