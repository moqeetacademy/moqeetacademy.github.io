Class 9 Physics Chapter 3 Notes — Dynamics NBF Syllabus

Chapter Overview: Chapter 3 of Class 9 Physics (NBF Syllabus) covers Dynamics — the branch of physics that deals with the causes of motion. Students study Newton's three laws of motion, inertia, momentum, the law of conservation of momentum, and the forces involved in everyday situations. The chapter also explains the difference between mass and weight, contact and non-contact forces, fundamental forces of nature, and the role of Dr. Abdus Salam in unifying two of these forces. These concepts are among the most heavily tested topics in FBISE and Punjab Board exams.

Short Questions and Answers

Q1. When a motorcyclist hits a stationary car, he may fly off the motorcycle, and the driver may get a neck injury. Explain why this happens.

This happens due to inertia, which is the property of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.

Flying Off the Motorcycle: When the motorcycle hits the stationary car and suddenly stops, the motorcyclist's body continues to move forward due to inertia. This causes the motorcyclist to fly off the motorcycle.

Neck Injury: The sudden stop causes the head to move rapidly forward and backward due to inertia. This abrupt movement can result in a neck injury, commonly referred to as whiplash. Both effects are explained by Newton's First Law of Motion.

Q2. In autumn, when you shake a branch, the leaves are detached. Why?

This happens due to inertia. When you shake the branch, the branch moves back and forth, but the leaves, due to their inertia, tend to remain in their original position. As a result, the force of the shaking causes the leaves to detach from the branch. The detachment of leaves occurs because the inertia of the leaves resists the motion of the branch.

Q3. Why is it not safe to apply brakes only on the front wheel of a bicycle?

When brakes are applied suddenly, the bicycle experiences a forward deceleration. Due to inertia, the rider's body tends to keep moving forward. If only the front wheel brakes are applied, the rear wheel may lift off the ground and the bicycle can flip forward, causing the rider to fall and get injured. To ensure safety, brakes should be applied evenly on both wheels to maintain balance and stability while stopping.

Q4. Deduce Newton's First Law of Motion from Newton's Second Law of Motion.

Newton's Second Law states: F = ma. If no external force acts on the object, then F = 0. Substituting into the equation:

0 = ma — since mass m cannot be zero, acceleration a must be zero.

When acceleration is zero, velocity remains constant. If the object is at rest, it stays at rest. If in motion, it continues in a straight line at constant velocity. This is exactly the statement of Newton's First Law — thus, the First Law is deduced from the Second Law.

Q5. Action and reaction are equal but opposite in direction. These forces always act in pairs. Do they balance each other? Can bodies move under an action-reaction pair?

Do they balance? No. Action and reaction forces do not balance each other because they act on different bodies. For forces to balance, they must act on the same body. Since action and reaction act on two separate objects, they do not cancel each other out.

Can bodies move? Yes. For example, when a person jumps, their legs push down on the ground (action), and the ground pushes up with an equal and opposite force (reaction), propelling the person upward. Motion is possible because action and reaction forces act on different bodies, allowing each body to experience a net force.

Q6. A man slips on an oily floor. He wants to move out of this area. He is alone. He throws his bag to move out. Why is it so?

This happens due to the principle of conservation of momentum. When the man throws his bag in one direction, the bag gains momentum in that direction. To conserve the total momentum (which was initially zero as both were stationary), the man experiences an equal and opposite momentum, causing him to move in the opposite direction. This is also an application of Newton's Third Law: "For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction."

Q7. How would you use Newton's Third Law and the Law of Conservation of Momentum to explain the motion of a rocket?

Newton's Third Law: When the rocket's engines expel exhaust gases downward at high speed (action), the rocket experiences an equal and opposite force upward (reaction). This upward force propels the rocket into motion.

Conservation of Momentum: The total momentum of the system (rocket + exhaust gases) is conserved. Initially, the system is at rest, so total momentum is zero. When the rocket expels gases downward, the gases gain downward momentum. To conserve momentum, the rocket gains equal momentum in the upward direction, explaining its continuous upward motion.

Q8. Why are cricket batter gloves padded with foam?

Cricket batter gloves are padded with foam to reduce the impact and force of the ball when it hits the gloves. The foam acts as a cushion, increasing the time of contact, which reduces the rate of change of momentum and therefore reduces the force (F = Δp/t). This absorbs the shock and protects the hands and fingers from injuries during play.

Q9. Where will your weight be greater — near Earth or near the Moon? What about mass?

Your weight will be greater near Earth because weight depends on gravitational force (W = mg), which is stronger on Earth due to its larger mass and higher gravitational pull (g = 9.8 m/s² on Earth vs. about 1.6 m/s² on the Moon). However, your mass remains the same near Earth and near the Moon because mass is an intrinsic property of matter and does not change with location or gravity.

Q10. When Ronaldo kicks the ball, at the highest point both Earth and the ball attract each other with the same force. Why then does the ball move towards Earth and not the Earth towards the ball?

The gravitational force between the Earth and the ball is equal in magnitude but opposite in direction (Newton's Third Law). However, the ball moves towards Earth instead of the other way around due to the vast difference in their masses. Using F = ma: for the same force F, the Earth's enormously large mass (6 × 10²⁴ kg) produces an acceleration so tiny it is completely undetectable, while the ball's small mass produces a clearly visible acceleration. Thus, the ball's motion is prominent due to its much smaller mass.

Long Response Questions and Answers

Q1. State Newton's First Law of Motion. Explain with examples. Why is it called the law of inertia?

Statement: A body at rest remains at rest, and a body in motion continues to move with a uniform velocity in a straight line, unless acted upon by a net external force.

Explanation with Examples:

  • State of Rest: A book lying on a table will remain there indefinitely unless someone applies an external force to move it.
  • State of Uniform Motion: A rolling ball on a rough floor slows down due to friction. On a perfectly frictionless surface, an object set in motion would continue moving forever.

Why is it called the Law of Inertia? It is called so because it describes the inherent property of matter called inertia — the natural resistance of any object to any change in its state of rest or uniform motion. The law establishes that an object cannot alter its state of motion by itself; it strictly requires an external net force to do so.

Q2. Define inertia. Why is it important to have knowledge of inertia in daily life? Elaborate with examples.

Definition of Inertia: Inertia is the characteristic property of matter by virtue of which it resists any change in its state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line. The mass of an object is a quantitative measure of its inertia — a greater mass implies a greater resistance to changes in motion.

Importance: Understanding inertia is crucial for human safety, mechanical engineering, and daily risk management. It helps us design protective equipment (seatbelts, headrests, airbags) and understand how massive vehicles behave during sudden braking or turning.

Daily Life Examples:

  • Passengers Leaning Forward During Sudden Braking: When a moving bus stops abruptly, the lower parts of passengers' bodies stop along with the bus, but the upper parts continue moving forward due to inertia.
  • Dusting a Carpet: When a carpet is beaten with a stick, the carpet fabric moves quickly but the dust particles remain at rest due to inertia and fall out.
  • Shaking a Tree Branch: When a branch is shaken, the branch moves back and forth, but heavy fruits and leaves maintain their state of rest due to inertia, causing them to fall.

Q3. State and prove Newton's Second Law of Motion. Deduce Newton's First Law from Newton's Second Law.

Statement: When a net force acts on a body, it produces acceleration in the direction of the net force. This acceleration is directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass of the body.

Mathematical Proof:

  • a ∝ F (when mass m is constant)
  • a ∝ 1/m (when force F is constant)
  • Combining: a ∝ F/m → F = kma
  • In SI units, k = 1, therefore: F = ma

Deduction of First Law from Second Law: From F = ma, if we substitute F = 0 (no external force): 0 = ma. Since mass m ≠ 0, therefore a = 0. Zero acceleration means the body either stays at rest or moves with constant velocity — which is exactly Newton's First Law.

Q4. State Newton's Third Law of Motion. Explain with daily life examples.

Statement: To every action, there is always an equal and opposite reaction. Action and reaction forces are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction, and they act on two different bodies.

Daily Life Examples:

  • Walking: Your foot pushes the ground backward (Action). The ground exerts an equal and opposite forward force on your foot (Reaction), propelling you forward.
  • Rocket Propulsion: Engines expel hot gases downward (Action). The gases exert an equal upward force on the rocket (Reaction), driving it upward.
  • Recoil of a Gun: The explosion pushes the bullet forward (Action). The bullet exerts an equal and opposite backward force on the rifle, causing it to kick backward (Reaction).

Q5. State the limitations of Newton's Laws of Motion.

Newton's laws accurately describe mechanics in our standard macroscopic world but have strict limitations under the following conditions:

  • Relativistic Speeds: Newton's laws assume mass is an absolute constant. However, as an object's speed approaches the speed of light (c ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s), its mass effectively changes. At these speeds, Einstein's Theory of Special Relativity must be used.
  • Subatomic Scale: For objects at the atomic or subatomic level (electrons, protons, quantum particles), Newton's laws fail. These systems are governed by Quantum Mechanics.
  • Non-Inertial Reference Frames: Newton's laws apply only in inertial frames (at rest or moving at constant velocity). They do not hold in accelerating or rotating frames unless fictitious forces (centrifugal, Coriolis) are added.

Q6. Differentiate between contact and non-contact forces. Also explain fundamental forces and Dr. Abdus Salam's role in unifying two of them.

FeatureContact ForcesNon-Contact Forces (Field Forces)
DefinitionForces that act only when there is physical contact between the objects.Forces that act through empty space or a field without requiring physical contact.
ExamplesFriction, tension, normal force, muscular force.Gravitational force, magnetic force, electrostatic force.

The Four Fundamental Forces:

  • Gravitational Force: Universal attraction between any two masses. Weakest force, infinite range.
  • Electromagnetic Force: Acts between electrically charged particles. Infinite range.
  • Strong Nuclear Force: Binds protons and neutrons in the nucleus. Strongest force, very short range.
  • Weak Nuclear Force: Responsible for radioactive decay. Short range.

Role of Dr. Abdus Salam: Dr. Abdus Salam, a Pakistani theoretical physicist, proved that the Weak Nuclear Force and the Electromagnetic Force are two manifestations of a single unified force at high energy levels — called the Electroweak Theory. For this historic achievement, he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1979, sharing it with Sheldon Glashow and Steven Weinberg.

Q7. Represent the forces acting on a body using free body diagrams.

A Free Body Diagram (FBD) is a simplified graphical representation used to isolate a single body and visualize all external vector forces acting upon it.

Steps to Construct an FBD:

  1. Isolate the object and draw it as a simple dot or box at the center.
  2. Draw every external force as a vector arrow originating from the center, pointing in the direction of the force.
  3. The length of each arrow represents the magnitude; label each arrow with its symbol (W, N, F, f).

Example — A block resting on a flat table:

  • A downward arrow: Weight (W = mg) pulling the block toward Earth.
  • An upward arrow: Normal Force (N) from the table surface, exactly balancing the weight.

Q8. Define momentum. What is its formula and unit? Is it a scalar or vector? Show that units Ns and kg·m/s are equal.

Definition:

Momentum is a measurement of the quantity of motion possessed by a moving object. It is the product of the mass of the body and its velocity.

Formula: P = mv — where P is momentum, m is mass, v is velocity.

SI Unit: kg·m/s (equivalent to N·s).

Quantity Type: Momentum is a vector quantity — it has both magnitude and direction (same direction as velocity).

Proof that N·s = kg·m/s:

  • From F = ma: 1 Newton = 1 kg × 1 m/s² = 1 kg·m/s²
  • N·s = (kg·m/s²) × s = kg·m/s
  • Therefore, N·s and kg·m/s are identical units.

Numerical Problems and Solutions

Q1. A boy is holding a book of mass 2 kg. How much force is he applying? If he moves it upward with an acceleration of 3 m/s², how much force should he apply?

Given: m = 2 kg, g = 9.8 m/s², a = 3 m/s²

Part 1 — Force while holding still:
F₁ = m × g = 2 × 9.8 = 19.6 N

Part 2 — Force while moving upward with acceleration:
F_total = m × (g + a) = 2 × (9.8 + 3) = 2 × 12.8 = 25.6 N

Q2. A girl of mass 30 kg is running with a velocity of 4 m/s. Find her momentum.

Given: m = 30 kg, v = 4 m/s

Formula: P = m × v

P = 30 × 4 = 120 kg·m/s (or 120 N·s)

Q3. A 2 kg steel ball moving at 15 m/s hits a bulk of sand and comes to rest in 0.2 seconds. Find the force applied by sand on the ball.

Given: m = 2 kg, vᵢ = 15 m/s, v_f = 0 m/s, t = 0.2 s

Formula: F = m × (v_f − vᵢ) / t

F = 2 × (0 − 15) / 0.2 = −30 / 0.2 = −150 N
(Negative sign indicates opposing force by sand on the ball)

Q4. A 100 g bullet is fired from a 5 kg gun. Muzzle velocity of bullet is 20 m/s. Find the recoil velocity of the gun.

Given: m (bullet) = 100 g = 0.1 kg, v (bullet) = 20 m/s, M (gun) = 5 kg

Using Law of Conservation of Momentum:
Initial momentum = 0 (system at rest)
M × V + m × v = 0
V = −(m × v) / M = −(0.1 × 20) / 5 = −2 / 5 = −0.4 m/s
(Negative sign indicates gun moves backward)

Q5. A robotic car of 15 kg is moving at 25 m/s. Brakes apply a constant force of 50 N. How long does the car take to stop?

Given: m = 15 kg, vᵢ = 25 m/s, v_f = 0 m/s, F = −50 N

Formula: t = m × (v_f − vᵢ) / F

t = 15 × (0 − 25) / (−50) = (−375) / (−50) = 7.5 s

⭐ Exam Tips for Chapter 3

Newton's Second Law (F = ma) and Momentum (P = mv) are the most tested formulas in this chapter. Always remember: impulse = F × t = change in momentum. For the gun-bullet recoil and rocket problems, use conservation of momentum (total momentum before = total momentum after). Also be ready to explain Dr. Abdus Salam's contribution — it frequently appears as a short question.
