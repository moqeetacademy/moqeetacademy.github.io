Class 9 Physics Chapter 4 Notes — Turning Effect of Forces NBF Syllabus

Chapter Overview: Chapter 4 of Class 9 Physics (NBF Syllabus) covers the Turning Effect of Forces — a chapter with very wide scope. Students learn about torque (moment of force), the principle of moments, center of mass, center of gravity, equilibrium and its two conditions, static and dynamic equilibrium, stability and ways to improve it, friction (types, advantages, disadvantages, and methods to reduce it), terminal velocity, and circular motion including centripetal force. These topics appear heavily in both short and long question sections of FBISE and Punjab Board exams.

Key Topics in This Chapter

  • Like and unlike parallel forces
  • Torque (moment of force) — formula τ = F × d
  • Principle of moments
  • Center of mass and center of gravity
  • Finding center of gravity of irregular shapes (plumb-line method)
  • Conditions of equilibrium (1st and 2nd condition)
  • Static and dynamic equilibrium
  • Stability — how to improve it
  • Friction — causes, types, advantages, disadvantages, methods of reduction
  • Rolling friction vs. sliding friction
  • Terminal velocity
  • Centripetal force and circular motion
  • Orbital velocity and satellite motion

Short Questions and Answers

Q1. Why is a long spanner used to open or tighten nuts of a vehicle's tyre? Why is an extra-long wrench not suitable for tightening?

A long spanner is used because it provides greater torque (τ = F × d) due to the increased moment arm length, making it easier to loosen or tighten nuts with less applied force. However, an extra-long wrench is not suitable for tightening because it may exert excessive torque, which can over-tighten the nut, damage the threads, or deform the parts being fastened.

Q2. Why are door knobs fixed at the edges of the door? What will happen if the door knob is at the middle of the door?

Door knobs are placed at the edge to maximize the torque applied when operating them. Torque (τ = F × d) is directly proportional to the distance (d) from the axis of rotation (the hinge). Placing the knob at the edge maximizes the moment arm, reducing the effort required to open or close the door. If the knob were at the middle, the shorter moment arm would reduce torque, requiring significantly more force to operate the door.

Q3. If you drop a feather and a bowling ball from the same height, which one reaches terminal velocity first? Which hits the ground first?

The feather reaches terminal velocity first because its large surface area relative to its low weight means air resistance has a much greater proportional effect on it — it reaches terminal velocity almost immediately. However, the bowling ball hits the ground first because it experiences relatively little air resistance for its mass and has a much higher terminal velocity, so it continues to accelerate for much longer.

Q4. Why do ice skates slide effortlessly on ice, while shoes cause skidding?

Ice skates slide effortlessly because the pressure exerted by the skater's weight on the thin blade lowers the melting point of the ice, forming a thin layer of water that acts as a lubricant and reduces friction. Shoes have a much larger surface area and cannot generate enough pressure to create this effect, resulting in more friction and skidding instead of smooth sliding.

Q5. Explain why it's easier to push a car on flat tyres than inflated ones. What happens to the frictional force?

Flat tyres increase the surface area of contact between the tyre and the road, reducing the pressure per unit area. This causes the tyres to deform more, which actually lowers rolling resistance. Since frictional resistance to rolling is primarily due to this deformation (rolling resistance), flat tyres reduce this resistance, making it easier to push the car forward despite the seemingly larger contact patch.

Q6. When standing on a crowded school bus, which stance gives better stability — legs joined or legs spread apart?

Standing with legs spread apart gives better stability. This stance increases the base of support, lowers the center of gravity, and makes it harder for external forces (such as sudden bus movements) to topple you. With legs joined, the base of support is narrow, the center of gravity is relatively higher, and balance is much easier to lose.

Q7. Why is a moving bicycle easier to balance? Relate this to the principle of rotational motion.

A moving bicycle is easier to balance due to the gyroscopic effect. When the wheels are spinning, they create angular momentum, which resists changes to their orientation. This stabilizing force helps keep the bicycle upright. At higher speeds, the angular momentum is greater, making balance even easier. A stationary bicycle has no gyroscopic effect, so it falls easily.

Q8. Why is a pencil standing on its tip unstable? What factors affect the stability of an object balanced on a point?

A pencil on its tip is unstable because its center of gravity (CG) is high and its base of support is extremely small. Even a slight disturbance creates a torque that tips it over, and since the CG falls outside the tiny base area, it cannot recover.

Factors affecting stability:

  • Height of CG: Lower CG improves stability.
  • Base of Support: Wider base increases stability.
  • Mass Distribution: Uniformly distributed mass enhances balance.

Q9. While driving, what happens if the driver takes the curve too fast? How does centripetal force keep the car on the road?

If the driver takes a curve too fast, the car may experience insufficient centripetal force to maintain the curved path, causing it to skid outward due to inertia. Centripetal force (directed toward the center of the curve) counteracts the car's tendency to move in a straight line. This centripetal force is provided by static friction between the tyres and road. If speed is too high, friction cannot provide sufficient centripetal force and the car skids off the road.

Q10. Consider a ball on a string swinging in a circle. How does tension in the string vary at different points in the circular path?

The tension in the string varies depending on the ball's position:

  • At the lowest point: Tension is maximum because it must counteract both the centripetal force requirement AND the weight of the ball (both act in opposite directions on the string).
  • At the highest point: Tension is minimum because the weight of the ball contributes toward the centripetal force, so less tension is required.

The forces involved are: tension in the string (toward center), gravitational force (downward), and centripetal acceleration (toward center of circle at all points).

Q11. Why is it important for communication satellites in geostationary orbit to maintain a specific speed?

Communication satellites in geostationary orbit must maintain a specific speed so that their orbital period matches Earth's rotation period (24 hours). This synchronization makes the satellite appear stationary relative to a specific point on Earth's surface, enabling continuous communication with fixed ground stations. If the satellite's speed deviates, it drifts out of position relative to Earth, disrupting communication links.

Long Response Questions and Answers

Q1. Differentiate between like and unlike parallel forces.

FeatureLike Parallel ForcesUnlike Parallel Forces
DirectionForces act in the same direction.Forces act in opposite directions.
Line of ActionParallel lines of action pointing the same way.Parallel lines of action pointing opposite ways.

Q2. What is torque (moment of force)? On what factors does it depend? Write its mathematical formula.

The turning effect of a force produced in a body about an axis of rotation is called the moment of force or torque.

Torque depends on two factors:

  • Magnitude of the applied force (F): Greater force produces greater torque.
  • Moment arm (d): The perpendicular distance from the axis of rotation to the line of action of the force. A longer moment arm produces greater torque.

Mathematical Formula: τ = F × d

SI Unit: Newton-meter (N·m)

Q3. Define center of mass. What is the effect of mass distribution on the center of mass?

The center of mass (COM) of a body is a specific point where an applied net force causes the system to move linearly without any rotation. It behaves as if the entire mass of the body is concentrated at that single point.

Effect of mass distribution:

  • Symmetrical/Uniform Distribution: In a uniform, homogeneous body (like a solid steel sphere), the center of mass lies exactly at its geometric center.
  • Asymmetrical Distribution: If more mass is concentrated on one side, the center of mass shifts toward the heavier side. For example, in a cricket bat, the COM is closer to the heavier hitting end, not the handle.

Q4. What is center of gravity? Where is the center of gravity of regular shaped bodies (circular plate, rectangular plate, triangular plate, cylinder, sphere)? Differentiate between center of mass and center of gravity.

The center of gravity (COG) is the single point at which the entire weight of a body appears to act vertically downward under the influence of gravity.

Location of COG for regular shapes (in a uniform gravitational field):

  • Circular Plate / Sphere: Exactly at the geometric center.
  • Rectangular / Square Plate: At the intersection of its diagonals.
  • Triangular Plate: At the centroid — intersection of its medians.
  • Cylinder: At the midpoint of its central longitudinal axis.

Differences between COM and COG:

  • Center of Mass depends purely on the distribution of mass — it remains the same anywhere in the universe, regardless of gravity.
  • Center of Gravity depends on the gravitational field. In a uniform field (like Earth's surface), COM and COG are at the same point. In a non-uniform field, they can differ.

Q5. How can you find the center of gravity of an irregular shaped thin sheet of plastic?

The center of gravity of an irregular thin sheet is found experimentally using the plumb-line method:

  1. Make holes: Drill 3 small holes near the edges of the sheet at different positions (A, B, and C).
  2. Suspend the sheet: Hang the sheet loosely from a pin through hole A so it can swing freely.
  3. Use a plumb-line: Suspend a plumb-line (string with a weight) from the same pin. Once it stops swinging, draw a straight vertical line along the string's path on the sheet.
  4. Repeat: Suspend through hole B, draw a second line. Repeat for hole C to draw a third line.
  5. Locate the intersection: The point where all three lines intersect is the Center of Gravity of the sheet.

Q6. What is equilibrium? Describe the conditions of equilibrium. State and explain the principle of moments.

A body is in equilibrium if it is either at rest or moving with uniform velocity — meaning there is no change in its state of motion.

Two Conditions for Complete Equilibrium:

  • First Condition (Translational Equilibrium): The vector sum of all external forces acting on the body must be zero. (ΣF = 0)
  • Second Condition (Rotational Equilibrium): The vector sum of all torques acting on the body about any axis must be zero. (Στ = 0)

Principle of Moments: If an object is in rotational equilibrium, the sum of all clockwise moments about a pivot is equal to the sum of all anticlockwise moments about that same pivot.

Sum of Clockwise Moments = Sum of Anticlockwise Moments

Q7. How can the stability of an object be improved? Illustrate with real-life applications.

Two primary methods to improve stability:

  • Lowering the Center of Gravity (COG): Keeping the heavy mass as close to the ground as possible.
  • Broadening the Base of Support: Widening the contact area with the ground so the vertical line from the COG falls safely within the base.

Real-Life Applications:

  • Racing Cars (Formula 1): Built very low to the ground with wide wheelbases to prevent flipping at high cornering speeds.
  • Double-Decker Buses: Heavy components (engine, chassis) are on the bottom deck; the upper deck is lightweight to avoid tipping on sharp turns.
  • Martial Artists / Weightlifters: Stand with feet wide apart and knees bent to widen their base and lower COG, making them harder to push over.

Q8. Define friction. What causes it? What are its advantages and disadvantages? How can friction be reduced?

Friction is the resisting force that opposes the relative motion or tendency of motion between two surfaces in contact.

Cause: At a microscopic level, all surfaces have tiny bumps and valleys (asperities). When two surfaces touch, these roughnesses interlock with one another, creating resistance to sliding.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Enables walking without slipping.Causes wear and tear in mechanical parts.
Allows vehicles to brake and stop safely.Generates unwanted heat energy.
Holds nails and screws firmly in walls.Reduces fuel efficiency of machines.

Methods to Reduce Friction:

  • Lubrication: Applying oil or grease to smooth out microscopic roughness.
  • Streamlining: Shaping objects (airplanes, sports cars) to minimize air resistance.
  • Ball Bearings: Converting sliding friction into much smaller rolling friction.

Q10. Analyse the dynamics of an object reaching terminal velocity.

When an object falls freely through the atmosphere, its speed changes through distinct phases:

  • Initial Fall: At the moment of release, velocity = 0, so air resistance = 0. Only weight (W) acts, causing maximum acceleration (g = 9.8 m/s²).
  • Increasing Speed: As the object speeds up, air resistance grows larger and opposes weight, reducing the net downward acceleration.
  • Reaching Equilibrium: The object accelerates to a speed where upward air resistance exactly equals downward weight.
  • Terminal Velocity: Net force becomes zero (ΣF = 0), acceleration drops to zero, and the object falls at a constant maximum speed called terminal velocity.

Q11. Define centripetal force. Describe the motion of a body in a circular path under centripetal force.

Centripetal force is the net inward force required to keep an object moving along a curved or circular path. It is always directed perpendicular to the velocity vector, pointing straight toward the center of the circle.

Under continuous centripetal force:

  • The speed of the body remains constant.
  • The direction of motion changes constantly at every point along the path.
  • Because velocity direction keeps changing, the body is constantly accelerating toward the center (centripetal acceleration).
  • If centripetal force were suddenly removed, the object would fly off in a straight line tangent to the circular path due to inertia.

Q12. Identify different sources of centripetal force in real-life examples.

Centripetal force is not a separate, standalone force — it is provided by other physical forces depending on the scenario:

  • Planetary Orbits: Gravitational force between the Sun and Earth provides centripetal force, keeping Earth in orbit.
  • Whirling a Stone on a String: Tension force in the string pulls the stone inward toward the center.
  • Car Turning a Corner: Static friction between the car's tyres and road surface pushes the vehicle toward the center of the curve.
  • Washing Machine Spin Cycle: The normal force of the drum wall pushes clothes inward, while water escapes out through the holes.

Q13. Define orbital velocity. How do scientists use it to launch satellites? What factors influence the required speed?

Orbital velocity is the specific horizontal speed required for a satellite to maintain a stable, circular orbit around a massive body without falling down or drifting into deep space.

How satellites are launched: Multi-stage rockets lift a satellite to a targeted altitude above Earth's atmosphere (to avoid air resistance). Once the target altitude is reached, the rocket tilts horizontally and fires its engines to accelerate the satellite to its precise orbital speed. At this speed, the rate at which the satellite falls toward Earth due to gravity matches the rate at which Earth's surface curves away — placing it in a state of perpetual free fall (orbit).

Factors that influence required orbital speed:

  • Mass of the Central Body (M): Heavier central body requires faster orbital speeds.
  • Orbital Radius/Altitude (r): Satellites closer to Earth need higher speed; distant satellites orbit more slowly. Note: The mass of the satellite itself does not affect the required orbital velocity.

Numerical Problems and Solutions

Q1. Calculate the torque acting on a spanner of length 20 cm to loosen a nut by a force of 50 N. If the same nut is to be loosened by a force of 100 N, what should be the length of the spanner?

Part 1 — Calculate Torque:
r₁ = 20 cm = 0.2 m; F₁ = 50 N
τ = F₁ × r₁ = 50 × 0.2 = 10 N·m

Part 2 — Find New Length:
F₂ = 100 N; τ = 10 N·m (same nut, same required torque)
r₂ = τ / F₂ = 10 / 100 = 0.1 m = 10 cm

Q2. A steel bar of length 1.0 m is balanced at its middle. Two masses m₁ = 0.6 kg and m₂ are suspended at distances 0.2 m and 0.3 m respectively from the pivot. Find mass m₂.

Given: r₁ = 0.2 m, r₂ = 0.3 m, m₁ = 0.6 kg

Applying Principle of Moments:
m₁ × r₁ = m₂ × r₂ (g cancels from both sides)
0.6 × 0.2 = m₂ × 0.3
0.12 = m₂ × 0.3
m₂ = 0.12 / 0.3 = 0.4 kg

Q3. Two masses — 250 g at 65 cm and 100 g at 80 cm — are hanging on a uniform meter rod pivoted at the 50 cm mark. Where should a third mass of 400 g be placed to balance the rod?

Pivot at 50 cm:
m₁ = 250 g at 65 cm → r₁ = 65 − 50 = 15 cm (clockwise)
m₂ = 100 g at 80 cm → r₂ = 80 − 50 = 30 cm (clockwise)
m₃ = 400 g at position x (anticlockwise)

Principle of Moments:
m₃ × r₃ = (m₁ × r₁) + (m₂ × r₂)
400 × r₃ = (250 × 15) + (100 × 30) = 3750 + 3000 = 6750
r₃ = 6750 / 400 = 16.875 cm (to the left of pivot)

Scale Position = 50 − 16.875 = 33.125 cm ≈ 33.1 cm

Q4. A car weighing 1200 kg enters a roundabout with a diameter of 60 m at a speed of 25 km/h. Calculate the centripetal force acting on the car.

Given: m = 1200 kg, diameter = 60 m → r = 30 m
v = 25 km/h = 25 × (1000/3600) = 6.944 m/s

Formula: Fc = mv²/r
Fc = (1200 × (6.944)²) / 30 = (1200 × 48.22) / 30 = 57864 / 30 = 1929 N
(Note: The textbook key gives 693.3 N, which corresponds to a velocity of ~15 km/h. Using the stated 25 km/h with proper SI conversion gives 1929 N.)

Q5. A geostationary satellite revolves around Earth in an orbit of radius 42000 km. Find its orbital speed.

Given: r = 42000 km; T = 24 hours = 86400 s

Formula: v = 2πr / T
v = (2 × 3.14159 × 42000) / 86400
v = 263893 / 86400 ≈ 3.054 km/s (or 3054 m/s)
