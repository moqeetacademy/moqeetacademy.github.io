Class 9 Physics Chapter 5 Notes — Elasticity and Pressure NBF Syllabus

Chapter Overview: Chapter 5 of Class 9 Physics (NBF Syllabus) covers two major topics — Elasticity and Pressure. The elasticity section introduces Hooke's Law, spring constant, elastic and plastic materials, and the force-extension graph. The pressure section covers the definition and formula for pressure, atmospheric pressure and its measurement with a barometer, how liquid pressure varies with depth (P = ρgh), and Pascal's Law with its application in hydraulic systems. These are core topics tested in both short and long question sections of FBISE and Punjab Board exams.

Short Questions and Answers

Q1. While walking on a trampoline, do you feel more pressure when you stand still or jump up and down? Why does pressure change with movement?

You feel more pressure when jumping on a trampoline because your body moves quickly and hits the surface with greater force upon landing. Since pressure is force divided by area (P = F/A), the increased impact force increases the pressure on the surface. Standing still exerts less pressure because there is no additional impact force — only the body's weight acts.

Q2. How does the shape of a thumb pin help it penetrate surfaces easily?

A thumb pin has a sharp, pointed tip with a very small surface area. When you apply force, the pressure becomes extremely high at the tip (P = F/A — small area produces very large pressure). This high localized pressure allows it to easily penetrate surfaces like walls or boards with minimal effort. The broad flat head on the other side distributes pressure over a large area so your thumb is not hurt.

Q3. If you blow up a balloon and tie it closed, why does it stay inflated even though you stop blowing? How does pressure play a role?

Blowing air into the balloon increases the air pressure inside it. When you tie the balloon, the compressed air is trapped. This trapped air pushes outward equally in all directions (Pascal's principle for gases), and the excess internal pressure over atmospheric pressure keeps the balloon stretched and inflated, even after you stop blowing.

Q4. Why is an inner airtight layer of a space suit designed to maintain a constant pressure around the astronaut?

In space, there is no atmosphere and therefore no external air pressure. The human body requires a certain pressure around it for normal physiological functions — blood circulation, breathing, and preventing body fluids from boiling. The airtight pressure layer in a space suit maintains a constant internal pressure equivalent to atmospheric conditions, protecting the astronaut from the vacuum of space and ensuring survival.

Q5. If a liquid has a density twice that of mercury, what will be the height of the liquid column in a barometer?

In a barometer, the pressure balancing equation is P = ρgh. For the same atmospheric pressure to be balanced, ρ × h must remain constant. Mercury (ρ_Hg) gives h = 76 cm at standard pressure. If the new liquid has twice the density (ρ = 2ρ_Hg), then the required height halves: h = 76/2 = 38 cm.

Q6. Why wouldn't we be able to sip water with a straw on the moon?

On Earth, when we suck on a straw, we reduce the air pressure inside the straw. The atmospheric pressure outside then pushes down on the liquid surface, forcing the water up the straw into our mouth. On the moon, there is no atmosphere and therefore no atmospheric pressure. Without this pressure difference, no force pushes the liquid up the straw, making sipping impossible.

Q7. How are we able to break a metal wire by bending it repeatedly?

When a metal wire is bent repeatedly at the same point, it experiences stress at that location over and over. This causes a phenomenon called metal fatigue — the repeated stress disrupts the internal crystal structure of the metal, creating microscopic cracks that grow with each bend. Eventually, the metal becomes brittle at that point and breaks even with a small applied force.

Q8. A spring with spring constant k is cut into two equal parts. One part is loaded with the same mass m. What will its spring constant be now?

When a spring is cut into half, its spring constant doubles. This is because a shorter spring is stiffer — it takes more force to produce the same extension in a shorter length. So if the original spring constant was k, each half will have a spring constant of 2k. This is because k is inversely proportional to the natural length of the spring.

Q9. Why do static fluids always exert a force perpendicular to the surface?

The particles in a static (non-moving) fluid are in equilibrium and experience equal pressure from all directions. A fluid at rest cannot sustain any shear (tangential) forces — if any tangential force existed, the fluid would simply flow to redistribute. Therefore, the only force a static fluid can exert on a surface is perpendicular (normal) to it, pressing directly outward. This ensures mechanical equilibrium and prevents sideways flow.

Q10. How can a small car lifter lift a load heavier than itself?

A small car lifter uses a hydraulic system based on Pascal's Law. A small input force is applied to a small piston, creating pressure in the enclosed fluid. Pascal's Law states this pressure is transmitted equally throughout the fluid. When this same pressure acts on a much larger piston, it produces a proportionally much larger output force (F = P × A). This is the principle of a force multiplier — a small force multiplied by the area ratio can lift an extremely heavy car.

Long Response Questions and Answers

Q1. Define elasticity and elastic limit. Show that a force may produce a change in size and shape of solids.

Elasticity: The property of a body by virtue of which it tends to regain its original size and shape after the deforming force is removed.

Elastic Limit: The maximum value of deforming force up to which a body completely recovers its original size and shape after the force is removed. If the force exceeds this limit, the body undergoes permanent deformation.

Force Producing Change in Size and Shape: When an external (deforming) force is applied to a solid, it alters the relative positions of its molecules, leading to measurable changes in physical dimensions:

  • Change in Size: Stretching a rubber band increases its length. Compressing a metallic sphere from all sides reduces its volume slightly.
  • Change in Shape: Pressing a soft rubber ball changes its shape from spherical to oval. Applying a shearing force to the top of a fixed block twists its structure without changing its volume.

Q2. What is Hooke's Law? Illustrate its applications. Also define and calculate spring constant.

Hooke's Law: Within the elastic limit, the stress applied to a body is directly proportional to the strain produced in it.

Mathematically: F = k · x (for a spring)

Where F is the restoring force, x is the extension or compression from the equilibrium position, and k is the spring constant. The negative sign in the full form (F = −kx) indicates the restoring force acts opposite to displacement.

Applications of Hooke's Law:

  • Spring Balances: Devices that measure weight based on the extension of a calibrated spring.
  • Automobile Suspension Systems: Shock absorbers use heavy-duty springs to cushion vehicles from road imperfections.
  • Mechanical Watches: Balance springs (hairsprings) store and release energy to regulate timing mechanisms.

Spring Constant (k):

  • Definition:
    The stiffness of a spring — the magnitude of force required to produce a unit extension or compression.
  • Formula: k = F / x
  • SI Unit: Newtons per metre (N/m). A higher value of k indicates a stiffer spring.

Q3. Draw and explain the force-extension graph for elastic solids.

When a progressive tensile force is applied to an elastic metallic wire, its behavior follows distinct phases:

Graph Region / PointBehavior
Region OA (Proportional Limit)The graph is a perfectly straight line through the origin. Force is directly proportional to extension — Hooke's Law is strictly obeyed.
Point B (Elastic Limit / Yield Point)Beyond A up to B, the material loses strict proportionality but retains elasticity. If the load is removed at B, the wire returns completely to its original length.
Region BC (Plastic Deformation)Stretched past B, the material enters the plastic region. Molecules shift permanently. When force is removed, a permanent extension (permanent set) remains.
Point D (Fracture / Breaking Point)The ultimate stress threshold where structural integrity fails completely and the material physically breaks.

Q4. Define and explain pressure. What is the effect of area on the pressure acting on a surface?

Pressure is defined as the normal force acting per unit surface area of an object.

Formula: P = F / A

SI Unit: Pascal (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m². Pressure is a scalar quantity because the force considered is strictly perpendicular (normal) to the surface.

Effect of Area on Pressure: Since P ∝ 1/A for constant force:

  • Small Area → High Pressure: A sharp pin easily penetrates a wooden surface because the hammering force is concentrated over a tiny area, generating very high localized pressure.
  • Large Area → Low Pressure: Military tanks use wide continuous tracks instead of wheels. This distributes the massive weight over a large area, drastically reducing ground pressure and preventing the vehicle from sinking into soft ground.

Q5. Explain atmospheric pressure and its units. How is it measured with a liquid barometer? Explain its construction and applications.

Atmospheric Pressure: The force per unit area exerted by the weight of the air column above a given point within Earth's atmosphere.

Units and Standard Values: 1 atm = 101,325 Pa = 760 mmHg = 1013.25 mb (at mean sea level).

Mercury Barometer — Construction:

  1. A thick-walled glass tube (~1 m long), sealed at one end, is completely filled with pure mercury.
  2. The open end is temporarily capped, carefully inverted, and submerged into a trough of mercury, then uncapped beneath the surface.
  3. The mercury column drops until the hydrostatic pressure of the mercury column equals the atmospheric pressure pushing down on the open trough surface.
  4. The empty space at the top of the sealed tube is a Torricellian vacuum. At standard sea level, the stable column height is 760 mm.

Applications:

  • Meteorology: Monitoring barometric trends helps forecast changing weather fronts.
  • Aviation Altimeters: Calibrated barometers in aircraft determine altitude based on the drop in atmospheric pressure with height.

Q6. Explain how atmospheric pressure varies with altitude. What weather changes does pressure variation indicate? What are the everyday applications of atmospheric pressure?

Variation with Altitude: As altitude increases, atmospheric pressure decreases because the amount of air above gets less, meaning less air mass exerts downward force.

  • At sea level: ~101.3 kPa
  • At the summit of Mount Everest: ~34 kPa — breathing is very difficult without supplemental oxygen.
  • Commercial aircraft at ~35,000 feet must artificially pressurize cabins because outside pressure is dangerously low.

Weather Indicators:

  • Rapid Drop in Pressure: Approaching low-pressure system — signals storms, heavy rain, and strong winds.
  • Gradual Rise in Pressure: High-pressure system — signals dry, clear, and stable weather.

Everyday Applications:

  • Drinking Straws: Sucking reduces pressure inside the straw; atmospheric pressure outside pushes liquid up into the mouth.
  • Syringes: Retracting the plunger creates a partial vacuum; atmospheric pressure pushes liquid into the barrel.
  • Suction Cups: Pressing the rubber cup expels air; atmospheric pressure holds the cup firmly against the smooth surface.

Q7. Show that liquid in a container exerts pressure equal to P = ρgh. What is the effect of depth on the pressure of a liquid?

Derivation of P = ρgh:

Consider an imaginary vertical column of liquid with uniform cross-sectional area A, extending to depth h, with fluid density ρ:

  • Volume of column: V = A × h
  • Mass of column: m = V × ρ = A × h × ρ
  • Weight (force) of column: F = m × g = A × h × ρ × g
  • Pressure at base: P = F / A = (A × h × ρ × g) / A

Canceling area A from both numerator and denominator:

P = ρ g h

Effect of Depth: Since ρ and g are constants for a given liquid, pressure is directly proportional to depth (P ∝ h). Pressure increases linearly as depth increases. This is why the lower walls and foundations of concrete dams must be built significantly thicker than the upper portions — they must withstand the much greater water pressure at the bottom.

Q8. State Pascal's Law. Describe the working principle of a hydraulic lift. What is meant by force multiplier?

Pascal's Law: When external pressure is applied to any portion of an enclosed, incompressible fluid at rest, the pressure is transmitted completely and equally to every other portion of the fluid and to the walls of the containing vessel.

Working Principle of a Hydraulic Lift: A hydraulic lift consists of two interconnected fluid-filled cylinders — one with a small piston (area a) and one with a large piston (area A).

  • A small input force f is applied to the small piston, generating pressure: P = f / a
  • By Pascal's Law, this pressure acts equally on the large piston: P = F / A
  • Equating: f / a = F / A → F = f × (A/a)
  • Since A/a > 1, the output force F is much greater than the input force f.

Force Multiplier: A force multiplier is any mechanical system that allows a small input force to be amplified into a larger output force. In a hydraulic lift, the area ratio (A/a) is the multiplying factor — it enables operators to lift very heavy vehicles with minimal effort.

Numerical Problems and Solutions

Q1. A spring has a spring constant of 8000 N/m. If a force of 500 N is applied, what will be the displacement of the spring?

Given: k = 8000 N/m, F = 500 N

Formula: F = k × x → x = F / k
x = 500 / 8000 = 0.0625 m

Converting: 0.0625 × 100 = 6.25 cm

Q2. In a force multiplier, the small piston has a diameter of 15 cm and the large piston has a diameter of 30 cm. If 250 N force is applied on the small piston, what force will be produced on the large piston?

Given: d₁ = 15 cm, d₂ = 30 cm, F₁ = 250 N

Using Pascal's Law (force scales with area, area ∝ d²):
F₂ = F₁ × (d₂/d₁)² = 250 × (30/15)² = 250 × 4 = 1000 N

Q3. A hydraulic car lift lifts a car of mass 1000 kg when 50 N force is applied on a small piston of radius 20 cm. Find the radius of the large piston.

Given: m = 1000 kg, F₁ = 50 N, r₁ = 20 cm
F₂ = m × g = 1000 × 9.8 = 9800 N

Using Pascal's Law: F₁/r₁² = F₂/r₂²
r₂² = r₁² × (F₂/F₁) = (20)² × (9800/50) = 400 × 196 = 78400
r₂ = √78400 = 280 cm
(Note: The textbook key states 78.4 cm, which is the result before taking the square root — the correct answer is 280 cm.)

Q4. A water column in a beaker is 70 cm high. Find the pressure of water. (Density of water = 1000 kg/m³)

Given: h = 70 cm = 0.7 m, ρ = 1000 kg/m³, g = 9.8 m/s²

Formula: P = ρ × g × h
P = 1000 × 9.8 × 0.7 = 6860 Pa = 6.86 kPa

Q5. How much force should be applied on an area of 20 cm² to get a pressure of 4500 Pa?

Given: A = 20 cm² = 20 × 10⁻⁴ m² = 0.002 m², P = 4500 Pa

Formula: P = F/A → F = P × A
F = 4500 × 0.002 = 9 N

⭐ Exam Tips for Chapter 5

The two most important formulas are k = F/x (Hooke's Law / spring constant) and P = ρgh (liquid pressure). For Pascal's Law numericals, always remember that force scales with area — and if diameters are given instead of areas, use F₁/d₁² = F₂/d₂². The mercury barometer construction and the derivation of P = ρgh frequently appear as long questions in FBISE and Punjab Board exams.
