Class 9 Physics Chapter 2 Notes — Kinematics NBF Syllabus

Chapter Overview: Chapter 2 of Class 9 Physics (NBF Syllabus) covers Kinematics — the study of motion without reference to its causes. Students learn about different types of motion, scalars and vectors, distance and displacement, speed and velocity, acceleration, equations of motion, and graphical analysis using distance-time and speed-time graphs. This chapter also covers freely falling bodies and gravitational acceleration — all frequently tested in FBISE and Punjab Board exams.

Key Topics in This Chapter

  • Rest and motion — relative concepts
  • Types of motion: translatory, rotatory, vibratory
  • Scalars vs. vectors — definitions and examples
  • Distance and displacement
  • Speed and velocity — uniform, variable, average
  • Acceleration and deceleration
  • Equations of motion (3 kinematic equations)
  • Distance-time and speed-time graphs
  • Freely falling bodies and gravitational acceleration (g = 9.8 m/s²)

Short Questions and Answers

Q1. What kind of motion does a Ferris wheel have, and what kind of motion do the riders experience?

  • Motion of the big wheel: The big wheel exhibits rotatory motion, as it rotates about a fixed axis without changing its position.
  • Motion of the riders: The riders experience circular motion, as they move in a circular path along with the wheel.

Q2. Give two situations in which displacement is zero but covered distance is not zero.

  1. Back and Forth Motion: If the boy moves back and forth along a straight path, eventually returning to his initial position, his displacement at the end is zero. However, the covered distance is not zero because he has traveled a certain distance in both directions.
  2. Circular Motion: If the boy moves along a circular path and returns to the starting point, his displacement is zero. However, the covered distance is not zero because he has traveled the circumference of the circle, which is a finite distance.

Q3. A stone is tied to a string and is whirling in a circle. What is the direction of its velocity at any instant?

The direction of the velocity of the stone at any instant is tangential to the circle at that point. This means the velocity is along a straight line that touches the circular path at the stone's position and is perpendicular to the radius of the circle at that point.

Q4. Is it possible to accelerate an object without speeding it up or slowing it down?

Yes, it is possible. This can happen when the object moves in a circular path at a constant speed. In such a case, the acceleration changes the direction of the object's velocity rather than its magnitude. This type of acceleration is called centripetal acceleration, and it always acts toward the center of the circle.

Q5. Can a car moving towards the right have the direction of acceleration towards the left?

Yes, it is possible. If the car is moving towards the right and slowing down, the acceleration will act in the opposite direction (towards the left). This type of acceleration is called negative acceleration or deceleration, and it reduces the speed of the car while it continues to move in the right direction.

Q6. Describe situations in which (a) acceleration is in the direction of motion, (b) acceleration is against the direction of motion, (c) acceleration is zero and the body is in motion.

(a) Acceleration in the direction of motion: A car speeding up on a highway after pressing the accelerator. The car's velocity and acceleration are in the same direction, causing the car to gain speed.

(b) Acceleration against the direction of motion: A cyclist applying brakes while moving downhill. The motion is downhill, but the acceleration due to braking is uphill, causing the cyclist to slow down.

(c) Acceleration is zero and the body is in motion: A car moving at a constant speed of 60 km/h on a straight road without any change in speed or direction.

Q7. Examine the distance-time graph of a motorcyclist, what does this graph tell us about the speed of the motorcyclist?

The given distance-time graph shows that the distance covered by the motorcyclist increases linearly with time. This indicates that the motorcyclist is moving with a constant speed, as the slope of the graph (which represents speed) is constant. The velocity-time graph will be a straight horizontal line at a fixed value, representing uniform motion.

Q8. Which controls in the car can produce acceleration or deceleration in it?

If the speed of a car is increasing, it is called acceleration — we use the accelerator pedal to increase the speed. If the speed is decreasing, it is called deceleration — we use the brakes to decrease the speed. Additionally, the steering wheel can change the direction of motion, which also contributes to acceleration because acceleration involves changes in velocity (speed or direction).

Q9. If two stones of 10 kg and 1 kg are dropped from a 1 km high tower, which will hit the ground with greater velocity? (Neglect air resistance)

When air resistance is neglected, both stones will hit the ground with the same velocity and at the same time. This is because the acceleration due to gravity (g) is independent of the mass of the object. The only factors affecting the motion are the height from which they are dropped and the gravitational acceleration, which are identical for both stones.

Q10. A 100 g ball is just released from rest and another is thrown downward with a velocity of 10 m/s. Which will have greater acceleration? (Neglect air resistance)

Both balls will have the same acceleration, which is equal to the acceleration due to gravity (g = 9.8 m/s²). The acceleration due to gravity is constant and acts equally on all objects, regardless of their initial velocity or whether they are simply released or thrown downward.

Long Response Questions and Answers

Q1. Differentiate between rest and motion. With the help of example, show that rest and motion are relative to the observer.

FeatureRestMotion
DefinitionA body is said to be at rest if it does not change its position with respect to its surroundings over time.A body is said to be in motion if it changes its position with respect to its surroundings over time.
SurroundingsThe reference objects or environment remain stationary relative to the body.The reference objects or environment change their relative position to the body.
Daily ExampleA book lying on a study table, a building rooted firmly to the ground.A flying bird, a moving car navigating down a road.

Relativity of Rest and Motion: Rest and motion are not absolute terms; they are completely dependent on the observer's specific frame of reference. An object can simultaneously be at rest and in motion when viewed from different perspectives.

Example: Consider a passenger sitting completely still inside a moving transit bus:

  1. With respect to a co-passenger sitting next to him, the passenger is at rest because their relative distance and position do not change.
  2. With respect to an observer standing outside on the roadside, the same passenger is in motion because the vehicle, along with its occupants, is continuously changing its position relative to the stationary trees and ground.

Q2. What are different types of motion? Define each type with examples from daily life.

Motion can be fundamentally classified into three primary categories:

1. Translatory Motion: Every particle of the body moves through exactly the same linear distance in the same interval of time. It is further sub-divided into:

  • Linear Motion: Motion along a straight line path. (e.g., A car driving down a perfectly straight highway, or an apple falling vertically straight down from a branch).
  • Circular Motion: Motion along a fixed circular path. (e.g., A toy train tracking along a circular loop, or the moon revolving around the Earth).
  • Random Motion: The irregular, disordered, or unpredictable zigzag movement of an object. (e.g., The erratic flight path of a butterfly, or the chaotic thermal movement of smoke particles).

2. Rotatory Motion: The spinning or turning motion of an entire body around a fixed axis that passes directly through the body itself. (e.g., A child's spinning top, the rotation of ceiling fan blades around their fixed central spindle).

3. Vibratory Motion: The repetitive back-and-forth or to-and-fro movement of an entire body or system about its mean (central structural) position. (e.g., The rhythmic swinging of a playground swing, the regular movement of a grandfather clock's pendulum, or the rapid vibration of a taut guitar string when plucked).

Q3. What are scalars and vectors? Give examples. How are vectors represented symbolically and graphically?

Scalar Quantities: Physical quantities that are comprehensively described exclusively by their magnitude (a numerical value) along with a corresponding measurement unit. They completely lack directional dependence. Examples: Mass (e.g., 5 kg), Time (e.g., 10 seconds), Temperature (e.g., 37°C), Speed, and Energy.

Vector Quantities: Physical quantities that fundamentally require both a magnitude (numerical value and appropriate unit) AND a specific spatial direction to make up their full physical meaning. Examples: Velocity (e.g., 20 m/s due North), Displacement, Applied Force, Acceleration, and Linear Momentum.

Vector Representation Methods:

  1. Symbolic Representation:
    • Bold Typeface: Representing the character in heavy bold print, such as F for force or v for velocity.
    • Arrowhead Notation: Placing a small directed arrow above the standard variable symbol, such as F or v.
  2. Graphical Representation: Graphically, a vector is mapped as a straight, directed line segment terminating with a clear arrowhead. The physical length represents the magnitude; the arrow direction represents the spatial orientation.

Q4. Define the term position. Differentiate between distance and displacement.

Position: Position describes the specific location of a particular object relative to a designated, fixed reference marker called the origin. It explicitly dictates how far and in exactly which direction an object is situated from that established reference base.

Q5. Differentiate between speed and velocity. Also define average speed, uniform and variable speeds, average velocity, uniform and variable velocities.

Speed vs. Velocity Core Distinction:

  • Speed: The time rate at which an object covers spatial distance. It is a scalar quantity. Formula: Speed = Distance / Time.
  • Velocity: The time rate of change of displacement of a body in a specific direction. It is a vector expression. Formula: Velocity = Displacement / Time.

Velocity & Speed Sub-Classifications:

  • Average Speed: The overall distance traveled divided by the aggregate time interval required to span that distance. Formula: v_avg = Total Distance / Total Time
  • Uniform Speed: A condition where a moving body covers precisely equal portions of distance across perfectly equal intervals of time.
  • Variable Speed: Occurs when an object covers unequal step distances during identical windows of time.
  • Average Velocity: The net total resultant displacement vector of a body divided by the total time elapsed from start to finish. Formula: V_avg = Net Displacement / Total Time
  • Uniform Velocity: Achieved when a body registers equal structural displacements over matching, consecutive intervals of time. Both the absolute scalar speed and the literal spatial direction remain perfectly invariant.
  • Variable Velocity: Manifests if the absolute scalar speed changes, the directional tracking shifts, or both dynamic elements alter simultaneously over time.

Q6. What are freely falling bodies? What is gravitational acceleration? Write down sign conventions for gravitational acceleration and three equations of motion of a freely falling body.

Freely Falling Bodies: An object descending down towards the Earth entirely under the uninhibited influence of planetary gravitational attraction — with all external resistive impedances (such as fluid air drag) treated as completely negligible — is defined as a freely falling body.

Gravitational Acceleration: The steady, uniform acceleration induced within any freely falling object purely due to the continuous gravitational pull exerted by the Earth. It is symbolized globally by the letter 'g'. Close to the Earth's average sea level, its standard operational constant is: g ≈ 9.8 m/s² (frequently approximated to 10 m/s² for clean algebraic computations).

Sign Conventions for 'g':

  • Downward Motion: When an object drops downward toward the Earth's core, its instantaneous speed increases uniformly; hence, the value of gravitational acceleration is treated as Positive (+g).
  • Upward Motion: When an object is projected vertically upward away from the ground, its speed reduces uniformly until it halts at peak altitude; hence, the acceleration parameter is designated as Negative (−g).

Kinematic Equations for Freely Falling Motion:

  • 1st Equation: v_f = v_i + gt
  • 2nd Equation: h = v_i·t + ½gt²
  • 3rd Equation: 2gh = v_f² − v_i²

Where: v_i = initial velocity, v_f = final velocity, t = elapsed time, and h = vertical height displacement.

Numerical Problems and Solutions

Q1. Convert the following expressions into standard physics units: (a) 160 km/h into m/s (b) 36 m/s into km/h (c) 15 km/h² into m/s²

(a) 160 km/h → m/s: 160 × (1000/3600) = 160 × (5/18) = 44.44 m/s

(b) 36 m/s → km/h: 36 × (3600/1000) = 36 × (18/5) = 129.6 km/h

(c) 15 km/h² → m/s²: [15 × 1000] / (3600)² = 15,000 / 12,960,000 ≈ 0.001 m/s²

Q2. In 10 seconds, a cyclist increases speed from 5 km/h to 7 km/h, while a car moves from rest to 20 km/h in the same time. Calculate and compare acceleration in each case.

Case 1 — Cyclist:
v_i = 5 km/h = 5 × (5/18) = 1.389 m/s
v_f = 7 km/h = 7 × (5/18) = 1.944 m/s; t = 10 s
a_cyclist = (1.944 − 1.389) / 10 = 0.055 m/s²

Case 2 — Car:
v_i = 0 m/s; v_f = 20 km/h = 20 × (5/18) = 5.556 m/s; t = 10 s
a_car = (5.556 − 0) / 10 = 0.556 m/s²

Comparison: The acceleration of the car (0.556 m/s²) is exactly 10 times greater than the acceleration of the cyclist (0.055 m/s²).

Q3. A ball is thrown straight up such that it took 2 seconds to reach the top after which it started falling back. What was the speed with which the ball was thrown up?

Given: t = 2 s; v_f = 0 m/s (at top); g = −9.8 m/s²
Using: v_f = v_i + gt
0 = v_i + (−9.8 × 2)
0 = v_i − 19.6
v_i = 19.6 m/s

Q4. A car is moving with uniform velocity of 20 m/s for 20 seconds. Then brakes are applied and it comes to rest with uniform deceleration in 30 s. Calculate the total distance covered.

Using speed-time graph (trapezium area):
Parallel Side 1 (total time base, 0 to 50 s) = 50 s
Parallel Side 2 (constant speed window, 0 to 20 s) = 20 s
Perpendicular Height (peak velocity) = 20 m/s

Distance = ½ × (Sum of Parallel Sides) × Height
= ½ × (50 + 20) × 20
= ½ × 70 × 20
= 700 meters

Q5. A girl starts on a racing bicycle at a speed of 50 km/h. Her speed is changing at a constant rate. If she stops after 60 s, what is her acceleration?

v_i = 50 km/h = 50 × (5/18) = 13.89 m/s
v_f = 0 m/s; t = 60 s
a = (v_f − v_i) / t = (0 − 13.89) / 60
a = −0.231 m/s² (Negative sign indicates deceleration/retardation)

Q6. Speed-time graph analysis: (a) Identify acceleration, deceleration, and zero acceleration parts. (b) Calculate covered distance from 10 s to 20 s.

(a) Segment OA (0s to 10s): Uniform Acceleration — slope rises linearly, speed increases from 0 to 50 m/s.
Segment AB (10s to 20s): Zero Acceleration — plot is perfectly flat horizontal, speed remains constant at 50 m/s.
Segment BD (20s to 30s): Uniform Deceleration — slope falls linearly, speed decreases from 50 m/s back to 0 m/s.

(b) Distance from 10s to 20s (Rectangle Area):
Δt = 20 − 10 = 10 s; v = 50 m/s
Distance = Base × Height = 10 × 50 = 500 meters

⭐ Exam Tip for Chapter 2

The three equations of kinematics are the most tested formulas in this chapter: v_f = v_i + at, s = v_i·t + ½at², and 2as = v_f² − v_i². For freely falling bodies, replace 'a' with 'g' and 's' with 'h'. Always take note of the sign convention for upward and downward motion.
